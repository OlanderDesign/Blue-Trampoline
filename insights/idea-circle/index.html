The Idea Circle

How might we become better at creating ideas by understanding what ideas are actually made of?

Thoughts
Ideation

Where do ideas come from?

Sometimes an idea arrives during a meeting. Sometimes while driving home. Sometimes halfway through a conversation or in the middle of a completely unrelated task. We often describe these moments as sparks of inspiration, as if ideas appear from nowhere and somehow choose us as their next destination.

Yet when I look back at ideas that have led to meaningful projects, changes, products, or improvements, they rarely seem to have appeared out of thin air.

More often, they seem to have emerged from a combination of things that were already there.

A problem that needed attention. A solution that became visible. An insight that connected pieces of knowledge. And occasionally an inspiration from somewhere entirely different.

Not as a process. Not as a formula. More as a way of understanding why some ideas emerge and others never do.

Most ideas begin with a problem, although we do not always recognise it as such. A problem can be obvious, like a customer frustration or a recurring operational challenge. It can also be subtle. A missed opportunity. A pattern that feels inefficient. A question nobody has answered yet.

The interesting part is that problems rarely exist alone.

When we spend time understanding them, they often reveal connections to other challenges. What initially looked like three separate problems may turn out to be symptoms of the same underlying issue. Equally, a single problem may be connected to customers, employees, processes, and strategy at the same time.

Instead of solving one isolated issue after another, we begin looking for solutions that create value in several places at once.

And that is where the second part of the circle enters.

Because when people hear the word solution, many immediately think of something new.

A new technology. A new platform. A new method. A new investment.

There is nothing wrong with new solutions. Many are valuable. Yet innovation has a habit of making novelty feel more attractive than familiarity.

The challenge is that every new solution arrives with its own baggage. New things require attention, learning, integration, maintenance, and trust. They solve problems while simultaneously introducing uncertainty.

What often gets overlooked is how many solutions already exist around us.

Organisations are full of underused capabilities, forgotten experiences, existing tools, and knowledge that has quietly faded into the background. Sometimes the most effective solution is not the newest one. It is the one we have failed to fully utilise.

The difference sounds small, but it can be significant. Improving something that already exists often creates momentum faster than introducing something entirely new.

Between problems and solutions sits what may be the most overlooked ingredient of all: insights. Not information. Insights. There is a difference.

Information is everywhere. Most organisations are surrounded by it. Reports, dashboards, project reviews, customer conversations, workshop notes, observations, and experiences are produced every day.

An insight appears when those pieces begin to connect.

When a customer comment suddenly explains a recurring operational issue. When a lesson from one project becomes relevant in another. When several observations point towards the same opportunity.

Insights are not created by collecting more information. They emerge when we create understanding.

This is why sharing knowledge matters. Why conversations matter. Why notes matter. Why reflection matters.

An organisation almost always knows more collectively than any individual inside it. The challenge is making that knowledge visible and useful.

Then there is inspiration. The part we talk about most. A book. A podcast. A conference. A customer visit. A conversation. An observation from another industry.

One of the most valuable skills in innovation is the ability to borrow perspectives from elsewhere. A hospital learns from aviation. A manufacturer learns from Formula 1. A software company learns from hospitality. New associations create new possibilities.

Yet inspiration feels different from the other ingredients. Sometimes we know exactly where it came from. Other times we do not.

An idea appears and we cannot trace the source. The connection seems to have formed somewhere below the surface, long before we became aware of it.

That is why I think of inspiration as the topping rather than the foundation. Useful. Powerful. Occasionally transformative.

But ideas can emerge without it.

Problems, solutions, and insights are harder to escape. Perhaps this is also why the Idea Circle never really stops turning. A new insight changes how we understand a problem. A problem reveals the need for a different solution. A solution generates new insights.

An unexpected source of inspiration connects elements that previously seemed unrelated.

The circle keeps moving.

And perhaps that is the real lesson.

Ideas are rarely isolated moments of creativity.

More often, they are the visible result of connections that were already forming beneath the surface.
