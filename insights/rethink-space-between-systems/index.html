Rethinking the Space Between Systems

Most organisations don’t lack systems — they lack connection between them. Orbit offers a way to see work as a whole by linking projects, people, and data across existing tools, reducing the hidden effort of navigating fragmented information.

Thoughts
Blueprint

I have been presented with the product Orbit, and it made me reflect on something most organisations recognise but rarely articulate clearly:

Not in a dramatic way, but in a quiet, accumulated sense.

Work lives across Excel sheets, emails, specialised systems, and local structures that have all been created with good intentions. Each of them solves something. Each of them holds part of the truth. But taken together, they rarely form a complete picture.

So a significant part of the work becomes navigating between them. Finding the right version. Recreating context. Aligning information that already exists, just not in the same place.

A McKinsey study estimated that employees spend close to 20% of their time searching for internal information or tracking down colleagues for context. That number is worth sitting with. It says something about how much of our effort goes into handling fragmentation rather than moving work forward.

This is where Orbit becomes interesting to look at.

What it brings into focus is the idea of connectivity — not as a technical feature, but as a way of understanding work. Projects, resources, competences, communication, and documentation are not separate elements to manage individually.

Instead of asking organisations to move into a new system, it connects to what already exists and presents it in a way that makes relationships visible. You can follow the work across layers, move from overview to detail, and see how things are linked without having to reconstruct the story yourself.

It becomes less about where something is stored, and more about how it connects to everything else. And over time, that begins to reduce a type of effort that is rarely named but widely felt — the work of keeping systems aligned with reality.

If that effort can be reduced, even slightly, it opens up something else. A clearer overview of what is actually happening. A better sense of how resources and competences are being used. And the ability to move across data layers without losing context along the way.

Orbit is one way of approaching that.

Not as a finished answer, but as a perspective on how connected work might look when the system reflects the reality instead of sitting beside it.

If that line of thinking is relevant, I’m open to continuing the conversation — especially around how someone like Orbit Online supports that kind of overview, while still allowing you to drill into the details when it matters.
