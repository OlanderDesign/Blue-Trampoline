The Idea Gap

How might we capture more ideas, before they disappear, and before we convince ourselves they’re not ready yet?

Thoughts
Ideation

Most organisations don’t have an idea problem. Spend a bit of time inside teams, projects, or even informal conversations, and it becomes obvious that people are constantly noticing things that could be improved. They see inefficiencies, missed opportunities, new directions. They connect dots between technologies, customers, and experiences. In many ways, ideas are everywhere.

The real problem sits somewhere else. Those thoughts rarely make it far enough to be worked on.

There is a small, almost invisible moment between having an idea and doing something with it. Not implementation. Not validation. Not building a business case. Just the act of getting it out of your head and into a form where it can be shaped, challenged, and developed.

If you look at how innovation is typically described, it is often visualised as a funnel. Many ideas enter at the top, and only a few make it all the way through to implementation. That part is well documented. Studies across industries often show that a large majority of new initiatives fail to deliver expected results, with estimates frequently pointing to 70–90% not meeting their original ambitions. The funnel is narrow by design.

But even that picture is incomplete. Because it assumes that ideas actually enter the funnel. Most don’t. They never make it out of people’s heads.

Strategyzer illustrates this clearly in The Invincible Company using venture capital data. Looking at early-stage investments, roughly 6 out of 10 lose money entirely, around 3 out of 10 deliver modest returns, and only a very small fraction generate outsized results. You cannot pick the winner in advance. You need idea volume, and you need to work with many options to uncover the few that matter.

At the same time, research from organisations like McKinsey points to another consistent pattern: even when strategies and ideas are defined, as many as 30–70% fail in execution. There is a persistent gap between thinking and doing.

Put those two observations together, and something becomes clear. We struggle both with getting ideas into the system and with carrying them through. But we tend to focus almost all our attention on the latter. In practice, the earlier problem is easier to miss.

People hesitate because the idea is not “ready”. They wait until it sounds smarter, clearer, more complete. Some capture it briefly, but never return to it. Others feel that if they cannot immediately explain the customer, the problem, and the value, then it is probably not worth pursuing.

The tools we typically use are not designed for this moment. A Business Model Canvas is powerful, but it assumes you already have something you can describe across customers, value, and revenue. Lean Canvas moves a bit earlier by focusing on problem and solution, but it still requires a level of clarity that early ideas rarely have. Most innovation frameworks, stage-gates, and governance models start from the same place: something that is already formed enough to be assessed.

Early ideas are not like that. They are incomplete, inconsistent, and often slightly irrational. That is exactly what makes them interesting.

Over time, different approaches have tried to get closer to this early stage. Rready’s Kickbox lowers the threshold and legitimises the messy beginning. Innovator’s DNA helps explain where ideas come from curiosity, questioning, connecting, but is less concrete when it comes to capturing them. Lean Canvas helps translate thinking into something more structured, once you are a bit further along.

And in many organisations, you see the same pattern repeat itself: people create their own simple formats. Idea cards. Opportunity cards. Quick prompts. Small ways of catching something before it disappears. That in itself says something.

We keep building tools to develop ideas, but the real friction sits earlier, in the transition from thought to something you can actually work with.

Part of this is structural. Most organisations are not designed to handle half-formed thinking. The formats we use expect clarity, logic, and direction. That is understandable, but it also means that early ideas have nowhere natural to go.

Part of it is psychological. We tend to overestimate how complete an idea needs to be before it is worth writing down. At the same time, we underestimate how much clarity only emerges after we start externalising it.

So we wait for the idea to become better before we give it a chance to become better.

The uncomfortable implication is that the quality of ideas is not only a function of how we develop them. It is also a function of how early we are willing to catch them.

Because if only a small fraction of ideas ever lead to meaningful outcomes, then losing ideas early is not neutral. It directly reduces your chances of finding the ones that matter.

And if many of the ideas that do enter the system still fail in execution, then the cost of never even starting is even higher than we tend to assume.

The question is not how to build better business cases. It is how to make it easier to take a vague thought seriously enough to put it down, and just structured enough to take the next step.

Because once something is on paper, it changes character. It becomes something you can react to. Adjust. Challenge. Share. It becomes something that can move.

Maybe that is where most innovation efforts quietly succeed or fail. Not in strategy. Not in execution. But in whether ideas ever make it across that small, invisible gap in the first place.

This is a starting point. Because if ideas never make it out of our heads, everything that follows becomes irrelevant.
